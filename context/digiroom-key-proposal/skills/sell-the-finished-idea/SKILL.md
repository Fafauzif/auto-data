# Sell the Finished Idea

Lead with the solution the judges should choose. Show how it works as a complete product, service, or strategy. Work backward from that end state to what the team needs to deliver it.

This applies throughout an active competition conversation, including brainstorming, progress replies, analysis, solution decisions, and revision. Write in the user's or deliverable's language. Apply the same rules to English, Indonesian, and mixed-language output. Adapt to the brief and the requested format.

## Mandatory rules

1. **Deliver the requested thing now.** A solution request gets the actual solution, a pitch request gets pitch-ready copy, a screen request gets actual screen content, and a build request gets the artifact within the available scope. A path to a deliverable belongs behind the deliverable. For a diagnosis request, give the finding and the strategic decision it changes. For multiple options, make each option tangible and choose or rank when the user asks.
2. **Describe the chosen design in the present tense.** Use concrete actors, actions, and outcomes. “The owner chooses a job. The branch confirms fitment, price, and the slot.” Write operating rules and customer-facing states as they work in the design. Use imperative or future tense where it naturally belongs in a rollout schedule. Neither tone implies a real deployment.
3. **Ban default pilot and validation framing in user-visible competition material.** Do not lead, end, or interrupt with “we need to test,” “the pilot should,” “subject to validation,” “needs validation,” “illustrative concept,” “just a prototype,” “proof of concept,” “proposed for consideration,” “masih perlu diuji,” “perlu divalidasi,” “sekadar simulasi,” or semantic equivalents. This applies to summaries, analysis recommendations, next steps, slide titles, speaker notes, captions, flows, charts, buttons, banners, and generated images or components. A synonym or softer version is still a violation if it makes the competition solution sound unfinished. Use these terms when the user explicitly requests them, the competition brief requires them, or the actual reported test is itself the factual evidence at issue. Keep such material to its relevant place.
4. **Put implementation after the product or strategy.** Start with the customer or operator experience and the business outcome. Then work backward to capabilities, owners, economics, first rollout scope, milestones, and success measures as relevant. A staged launch with specified deliverables is valid. Do not recast it as an unsolicited experiment, approval loop, research plan, or list of prerequisites for deciding what the product is. Quality assurance for what you deliver stays backstage.
5. **Keep claims accurate at the point where accuracy matters.** Speak decisively about what the design does and label important figures as evidence, targets, forecasts, or assumptions as applicable. Never invent commercial results, quotes, data, contracted partners, active integrations, price or stock verification, approvals, or deployments. Keep essential qualifications next to the affected claim or in a compact source or assumptions note. Keep the ordinary pitch and core customer flow free of repeated status reminders. If the user asks whether something actually exists or works live, answer directly.
6. **Use real product states on demo screens.** Use labels a real customer would need such as “Request received,” “Awaiting branch confirmation,” “Estimate updated,” or “Price unavailable.” Inspect rendered screens and visible strings, including empty states, dialogs, footers, and exported artifacts. Use coherent sample data if real data is unavailable. Keep any disclosure needed to prevent confusion about external actions factual and narrowly placed. A working control that saves a booking request does not manufacture a branch confirmation, and a demo flow must not transmit real requests or payments unless authorized.

## Build the argument

- Open with the business problem and the single decision that matters. State it at the level of a customer choosing between real alternatives. If the opposite of an insight is absurd, the insight is probably generic. Tie sharp findings to the segment, mechanism, and actual business choice.
- Name and define an idea in one sentence if a name helps. Show a representative user completing the journey. Specify who acts at each decisive step, the offer or decision they see, and the handoff that makes completion possible. Handle one realistic exception if it changes the outcome. Expand only as far as the assignment needs.
- Explain why the client can deliver the proposition using actual assets, relationships, or a clear operating model. Treat frameworks as supporting material. Let customer choices and business outcomes carry the headline.
- Tie the mechanism to a commercial or other case outcome. Name the event that counts. A feature, form submission, booking request, or app open has its own role in the journey; the business outcome occurs at the corresponding completion event.
- Backcast when implementation belongs in the deliverable. Name the required capabilities or commitments, the owner of each critical handoff, what each rollout wave releases, and the metrics, dates, or budget the brief needs. Where an external commitment is required, specify who secures it and when. Where data is missing, explain the consequence briefly alongside the relevant decision and keep building with reasonable assumptions. A current fact the user explicitly asks for still needs a factual answer.
- Preserve the user's chosen strategy, names, copy, or scope when editing. Remove weakening copy throughout the deliverable without dropping important evidence boundaries, true operating states, or required material.

## Match the format

- **Chat and strategy:** Start with the answer or decision. Show what the solution looks like using a concrete moment. Give only the rationale or rollout requested.
- **Executive summary and deck:** Sequence business finding, proposition, in-use experience, commercial logic, then delivery. Make headings assert something the judge needs to know. Include sources where they substantiate the argument.
- **Demo, mockup, or app:** Put the user into the product immediately. Use credible controls, names, content, and completion states. Remove showcase labels that a customer would never need.
- **Roadmap and operations:** Specify releases, owners, timing, costs, dependencies, and KPIs. Include experiments or evaluation design when requested or required by the brief.
- **Evidence and financials:** Bound observations to their actual sources. Use scenarios or targets where suitable and give the material drivers. Never claim an outcome was measured when it was modeled.

## Check before delivery

Read the first headline, first paragraph, user-facing surfaces, and final paragraph as a judge would. Fix any opening that starts with what the team should investigate. Remove process caveats and stand-ins such as “improve convenience” where the actual mechanism can be named. Confirm that the chosen idea, representative customer experience, and business completion event are clear. Keep any necessary caveat precise and in its proper place. If the request includes implementation, confirm it works backward from the finished solution.

Read [the distilled examples](references/competition-examples.md) for style calibration on solution writing, implementation, UI labels, and evidence. Transfer the writing and reasoning pattern. Reuse case facts only for the matching case and verify them against that case's materials.
